Canada Trade Surplus Hits Four-Year High

Canada posted its highest trade surplus since May 2022, driven by gold and energy exports.

By Y-Square Research / 2026-11-04 / …

Key points

  • Surplus hit C$5.35 billion in September.
  • Gold and energy drove the gain.
  • US share of exports topped 70%.
  • Excluding gold and energy, exports fell 5.6%.
  • Tariff talks have intensified.
CANADA TRADE SURPLUS HITS FOUR-YEAR HIGH

Canada's trade balance has fluctuated under tariff pressure, with gold and energy exports providing offsetting strength. The US share of Canadian exports has been volatile, crossing 70% for the first time in 13 months. Tariffs imposed by the Trump administration have hit consumer goods, electronics and industrial machinery. The war in Iran has raised crude oil and product prices, boosting Canadian export revenue.

Canada's exporters have adapted over months of US tariffs, with monthly trade data serving as a key indicator. The Canadian dollar and bond yields respond to trade surprises. Analysts had expected a smaller surplus. Excluding gold and energy, overall global exports dropped 5.6%, showing underlying weakness. Total imports fell 0.3%, led by motor vehicles and consumer goods. Exports to countries other than the US fell 1.1%. The trade relationship remains central to Canada's economy.

THE SEPTEMBER DATA

Canada posted a trade surplus of C$5.35 billion in September, the highest since May 2022, data showed on Wednesday, helped by gold and higher energy exports primarily to the United States. Higher shipments of these two categories south of the border increased exports to the US by 0.7% in September, widening Canada's trade surplus with its biggest trading partner to C$12.3 billion, from C$12.1 billion in August, Statistics Canada said.

The share of Canada's exports headed to the US exceeded 70% for the first time in 13 months. Excluding gold and energy, Canada's overall global exports in September dropped 5.6%, hurt by a new set of tariffs imposed by President Donald Trump. In volume terms, exports were down 4%. Analysts polled by Reuters had forecast a trade surplus of C$4.10 billion in September, slightly down from an upwardly revised C$4.95 billion in August.

The monthly goods trade number is an important indicator of how Canadian exporters are faring and adapting after months of US tariffs. It also shows how the war in Iran has helped Canadian export revenue due to higher crude oil and product prices. Exports of metal and non-metallic mineral products increased 8.6% in September, led by exports of unwrought gold which surged 16.8% and contributed the most to the monthly gain. Exports of energy products jumped by 8.2%.

StatsCan said that many export categories such as consumer goods, electronic and electrical equipment and parts and industrial machinery showed significant decreases as these were hit by Trump's new tariffs. Trump, whose Republican party lost control of both chambers of Congress in Tuesday's mid-term elections, slapped a set of new tariffs on Canadian exports in September after trade talks collapsed. However, this week both countries indicated that talks for a renewed trade deal have intensified. Total Canadian imports dropped 0.3% in September led by motor vehicle and parts and consumer goods. Exports to countries other than the United States fell 1.1% in September while imports dropped 1.4%, StatsCan said. The Canadian dollar was trading 0.57% higher at C$1.3930 to the US dollar, or 71.79 US cents. Yields on Canada's 2-year government bonds were down 0.8 basis point to 2.656%.

A MIXED PICTURE

Whether the surplus proves consequential depends on whether gold and energy strength can offset tariff-hit sectors. Consequential reasoning points to a mixed picture: the headline surplus beat forecasts and lifted the currency, suggesting resilience in commodity exports. Yet the 5.6% drop excluding gold and energy reveals underlying damage from tariffs. The US share crossing 70% indicates concentration risk.

If tariff talks intensify and succeed, non-commodity exports could recover. If talks fail, the drag on manufacturing and consumer goods may deepen. The war in Iran has boosted energy revenue, but that is externally driven and may not last. The market reaction was modest, with yields slightly lower. The outcome will influence whether Canada's economy can withstand prolonged trade pressure. The data suggest a two-speed export sector.

VOLATILITY AHEAD

If consequences materialize, the most likely outcome is continued volatility in Canada's trade balance, with commodity exports masking weakness elsewhere. That scenario depends on gold and energy prices remaining elevated and on tariff talks producing relief. The condition that would change it is a collapse in commodity prices or a deepening of tariffs.

A second plausible path is a gradual recovery in non-commodity exports if a trade deal is reached. The decisive variable is whether the US and Canada can convert intensified talks into an agreement. European governments will also watch whether North American trade fragmentation deepens. The outcome will shape not only bilateral relations but also Canada's growth trajectory. The September surplus is a headline gain with underlying fragility.
