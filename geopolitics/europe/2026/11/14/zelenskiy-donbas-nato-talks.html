NATO Ties to Donbas Withdrawal Floated in US-Russia Talks

US negotiators raised linking NATO membership to a Ukrainian withdrawal from Donbas; Zelenskiy said he would put any territorial concession to a national referendum.

By Y-Squre Research / 2026-11-14 / …

Key points

  • US negotiators raised the idea of Ukraine withdrawing from Donbas in exchange for NATO membership, the first time the linkage has been tabled in the talks.
  • Zelenskiy said he would put any territorial concession to a national referendum if Kyiv receives security guarantees, a funded army and reconstruction funds.
  • He rejected sequencing wider European security talks ahead of terms to end the war, saying the war must be ended first.
  • Two days of US-brokered talks in Abu Dhabi ended without a major breakthrough; a prisoner exchange of more than 1,000 people was agreed.
  • European leaders in Dublin agreed a five-power European team for any Ukraine peace talks, alongside EU officials.

A Bargain Raised Behind Closed Doors

KYIV, Nov 14 - US negotiators have floated the idea of Ukraine's potential withdrawal from its eastern region of Donbas in exchange for joining NATO, President Volodymyr Zelenskiy said, following the latest round of peace talks with Russia in Abu Dhabi. This is the first time the question of linking NATO membership to territorial concessions has been raised during the talks, Zelenskiy said in remarks to reporters released by his office on Saturday.

"The Americans raised a question: 'What if Ukraine joins NATO? Would Ukraine withdraw from Donbas?'" he said. "That is why I believe NATO is back on the agenda. But you have also heard (Russian President Vladimir) Putin's signals, and this is an extremely difficult conversation." Putin has long said Ukrainian membership of NATO is a red line for Moscow, citing the issue as one reason for launching Russia's full-blown invasion in 2022.

Zelenskiy reiterated his position that Kyiv was not ready to concede territory but said that if it received a comprehensive package including security guarantees, the right to a strong and well-funded Ukrainian army and funds to rebuild, he would put the question to a national referendum. Echoing comments made by European leaders at a summit in Dublin on Friday, he dismissed Russia's call for a broad agreement on European security architecture to be reached before the specifics of ending the war are agreed.

What Was Said and What Was Not

"We cannot turn everything upside down and start by talking about changing the security framework in the world or across the continent overall, and only then talk about ending this war. This war must be ended first," he said. US President Donald Trump's envoy and son-in-law Jared Kushner said on Saturday that the two sides had set out "maximalist positions" in the talks this week, but in private discussions both "understand that practical compromises will be necessary to end this war." Kushner added that "serious and constructive work continues among those of us committed to finding solutions."

The territorial question is not new to the table, but attaching it to NATO membership is. For Kyiv, a withdrawal from Donbas would cross a line written into its constitution and repeated in public statements since 2022; for Moscow, NATO expansion to include Ukraine has been the stated casus belli. A US-mediated swap of land for an alliance card therefore touches both sides' founding positions at once, which is why Zelenskiy reached for the referendum device rather than a direct yes or no.

A Referendum Route with Many Moving Parts

Whether it becomes consequential depends on three drivers. First, whether Washington is prepared to extend an Article 5-style guarantee to territory it would be defending, since a NATO pledge is only as credible as the forces behind it. Second, whether European capitals, now Ukraine's largest financial and military backers, will underwrite the reconstruction bill that a deal of this size implies.

Third, whether the Ukrainian public can be brought to ratify a loss of land, a step no government in Kyiv has yet accepted in advance. A proposal floated in a closed room is not a commitment, and both capitals have walked away from comparably shaped ideas before.

There is a plausible path in which none of this holds. A referendum can be deferred indefinitely, a security package can be watered down to equipment pledges, and the linkage can be dropped quietly in the next round if either side balks. Talks in Abu Dhabi already ended without a major breakthrough, and a prisoner exchange involving more than 1,000 people is the only concrete output so far.

The Most Likely Next Step

The most probable outcome is that the linkage survives as a negotiating position rather than a signed term, and that the next round of trilateral talks opens with a working document that pairs a ceasefire sequence with a security-guarantee schedule, leaving the territorial question to a later stage.

That scenario holds only if the United States keeps the proposal on the table and if Kyiv can show its public a guarantee package substantial enough to make a referendum conceivable; without those two conditions, the idea is likely to be filed alongside earlier frameworks that were raised and then shelved.
