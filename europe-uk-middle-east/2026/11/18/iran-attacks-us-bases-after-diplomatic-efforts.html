Iran strikes US bases in Gulf despite diplomacy

Gulf states intercepted Iranian drones and missiles after US strikes on Qeshm Island, as Trump weighed a threat to Washington and mediation continued.

By Y-Squre Research / 2026-11-18 / …

Key points

  • Gulf states said they intercepted missiles and drones from Iran and its Houthi allies in Yemen.
  • US fighter jets struck a Revolutionary Guards missile launch site on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Kuwait reported its first Iranian attack since September; Bahrain intercepted attacks for the third time this week.
  • Trump said the US took seriously a possible dirty-bomb plot; Rubio said there was no imminent threat to Washington.
  • Qatar and Oman continued mediation while Hormuz stayed partly blocked and tanker attacks increased.

US Strikes Qeshm Island and Gulf Airspace

The war entered a sharper phase this week when US fighter jets struck a Revolutionary Guards missile launch site on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz. The strike followed earlier Iranian attacks on US personnel and assets, and it put a major maritime chokepoint under fresh pressure.

Qeshm Island sits close to the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which about one-fifth of global energy supplies pass. A renewed round of strikes there raised immediate questions about oil flows and maritime security.

Gulf states then moved quickly to protect their own skies and ports, a pattern followed across the EU, UK and Middle East region. Kuwait, Bahrain, Saudi Arabia and other members of the Saudi-led coalition said they intercepted missiles and drones launched from Iran and its Houthi allies in Yemen. The exchanges added to a running tally of attacks across the southern Gulf since the conflict began in February.

Small shipping incidents have compounded the pressure. A liquefied petroleum gas tanker crew abandoned a vessel off Oman's Khasab following a surge in tanker attacks since October. The episode highlighted how quickly commercial traffic can be caught in the widening exchanges between Iran, its Houthi allies and the states around the Strait of Hormuz.

What the Gulf Reported On Wednesday

Bahrain said it intercepted Iranian aerial attacks for the third time in the week. It accused Tehran of maintaining a systematic hostile approach in a country that hosts the US Navy's Fifth Fleet. Bahrain's statement marked one of the clearest public warnings yet from a frontline Gulf state to Iran.

Kuwait reported its first Iranian attack on an oil-rich territory since September. The strike damaged or hit installations near Ali Al Salem base, the same site shown in satellite imagery released by Reuters. An Iranian army statement said it had targeted US drone and missile installations there after the US ally said its air defences were countering hostile drone attacks.

The Saudi-led coalition destroyed a cruise missile launched by the militia toward the Saudi capital of Riyadh. On the opposite side of the Gulf, US fighter jets fired on a Revolutionary Guards missile launch site on Qeshm Island. A US military official said the strike responded to Iran's attacks on US personnel and assets. Iranian media cited an Iranian official as saying the US attack did not damage infrastructure.

President Donald Trump said on Tuesday the US was taking seriously the possibility that Iran could be planning an attack on Washington. Asked about a CNN report citing US intelligence on a potential dirty bomb plot, he emphasised the risk. State Department spokesperson Marco Rubio said on Wednesday he did not think there was an imminent threat to the US capital.

Iran's Foreign Ministry denied the allegations, calling them baseless. A dirty bomb uses conventional explosives to disperse radioactive material and can spread contamination without producing a nuclear explosion.

Will the Mediation Lane Hold?

There are diplomatic efforts under way to end a conflict that began when the US and Israel struck Iran on February 28 and has since widened to include Iranian strikes on Israel and US assets in the Middle East. That conflict has now produced a new strand of fighting across the Gulf, with both sides trading blows near oil infrastructure and major shipping lanes.

Qatari and Iranian officials met on Tuesday for talks aimed at narrowing the gap between proposals on the ground. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araqchi later said in a Telegram post that he met Oman's Sultan Haitham bin Tariq and informed him about Tehran's diplomatic initiative to end the war. Oman has previously held talks with Tehran on controlling traffic in the Strait of Hormuz, a sticking point in negotiations.

The diplomatic signals have not stopped the military exchanges. On the morning of the reported attacks, the US struck a Revolutionary Guards launch site, while Gulf air defences responded in kind. Talks and strikes are now running on parallel tracks, one aimed at reducing the risk of a wider regional war and the other aimed at raising the cost of the existing one.

That split makes the coming days decisive. Both sides are signalling that they can escalate, but neither appears ready to accept a full return to the pre-war status quo. Any lasting pause will depend on whether the talks inside and outside the Gulf can translate the current restraint into a defined off-ramp.

Most Likely Outcome: a Stalled Chokepoint

The most likely outcome is a continued cycle of limited strikes and air defences rather than a full closure of the Strait of Hormuz. Cutting off the strait completely would disrupt about one-fifth of global energy supplies and close a route that several Gulf producers and their allies rely on for exports. That step would be far too costly for the parties involved.

Instead, the pattern will probably stay just short of a total blockade. Iran will keep using its Houthi allies to threaten shipping, while the US and Gulf states respond with targeted strikes on missile and drone capabilities. Each side will try to show it can defend itself without conceding control of the waterway.

The test for everyone watching is whether the talks inside and outside the Gulf can convert the current restraint into a defined off-ramp. If they succeed, the Gulf may see fewer attacks over the following weeks. If they fail, the region could see more frequent incidents near Hormuz and the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, with the energy markets watching every reported clash closely.

The immediate priority is protecting civilian shipping and preventing a mistake that turns an already tense standoff into a wider war. The Gulf states, the US and Iran have repeatedly shown they can de-escalate after moments of high tension, but the balance is delicate and depends on clear signals from both sides.
