Italy Removes Fascist Images From Parliament Exhibition

Italy's lower house ordered fascist imagery removed from an exhibition honouring Pino Rauti after opposition lawmakers objected.

By Y-Squre Research/2026/11/18/…

Key points

  • Italy's lower house ordered fascist imagery removed from a Rauti exhibition.
  • The display marked the centenary of the far-right figure's birth.
  • Opposition lawmakers said the show rewrote history.
  • The government defended it as a record of a lawmaker's life.

THE EXHIBITION AND THE OBJECTION

Italy's Chamber of Deputies ordered the removal of fascist imagery from an exhibition honouring Pino Rauti after opposition lawmakers objected. The banners and portraits were gone by Wednesday morning. The decision came after a morning of protests inside the chamber, with opposition members demanding that the entire display be taken down.

The show marked the centenary of Rauti's birth and was sponsored by the culture ministry. It included video, audio and material from his family archive. The exhibition had opened only days earlier, and its title promised a portrait of the man, the lawmaker and the intellectual.

Among the disputed images was a photograph of Rauti speaking beneath a flag bearing a Celtic cross, a symbol widely associated with neo-fascist groups. Portraits of Mussolini were also displayed. The Celtic cross has been used by far-right groups across Europe, which is why its presence in a state building drew immediate objections.

Alfredo Bazoli, a senator from the centre-left Democratic Party, said he came away incredulous. He described the show as a celebration rather than an exhibition. His mother died in the Brescia bombing, one of the deadliest attacks of the Years of Lead, and he has spoken about it publicly before.

The lower house administration acted after those protests, removing the pictures without cancelling the wider display. The compromise satisfied no one completely. The compromise satisfied no one completely, and both sides said they would keep pressing their case in the coming days.

RAUTI'S RECORD AND THE HISTORICAL DISPUTE

Rauti died in 2012. He volunteered for the Italian Social Republic, the German-backed state led by Mussolini in northern Italy during the Second World War. The Italian Social Republic existed only in the final years of the war, after Germany occupied northern Italy following the armistice of 1943.

He later became a leading figure in the Italian Social Movement, a party founded by supporters of Mussolini and regarded as a precursor of Giorgia Meloni's Brothers of Italy. The party later evolved through several names and mergers before its tradition was absorbed into the coalition that now governs the country.

In the 1950s, he founded the Centro Studi Ordine Nuovo, from which a group emerged that played a prominent role in extremist violence during Italy's Years of Lead. The group was linked to some of the most notorious attacks of the period, including bombings that killed dozens of civilians.

Rauti was investigated over some of those attacks but was cleared of all charges. That legal outcome does not settle the historical argument over his legacy. The acquittal ended the criminal case but left the historical record contested, which is exactly what the exhibition has now reopened.

Bazoli's mother was killed in a 1974 bombing in Brescia linked to Ordine Nuovo. He called the exhibition a blatant attempt to rewrite history, a phrase that framed the dispute far beyond the choice of images. For families of the victims, the display felt like an official endorsement of a man connected to the violence that took their relatives.

DEFENCE FROM THE CENTRE-RIGHT

Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani defended the show, saying Rauti deserved respect as a former lawmaker. He served five consecutive terms in the lower house until 1992. Tajani's argument rests on the idea that a democracy can examine its own past without endorsing it, a position his critics reject.

We live in a democracy, Tajani said, arguing that the display belonged within a free historical debate. He did not address the specific symbols that prompted the objection. He did not address the specific symbols that prompted the objection, leaving the central question of the dispute unanswered.

Federico Mollicone, a Brothers of Italy lawmaker who chairs the lower house culture committee, said the project was intended only to recount Rauti's path. Mollicone has defended similar projects before, arguing that the post-war right deserves a place in the country's official historical record.

He described the exhibition as the history of a tradition that existed and left a legacy. The wording angered critics who see that legacy as unresolved. Critics say the word legacy is doing a lot of work in that sentence, given the violence associated with the movement he describes.

Meloni has not commented publicly. She praised Mussolini in her youth but has since said she never felt sympathy for fascism and that Brothers of Italy has no place for nostalgia of that kind. Her silence has been noted by both sides, with opponents reading it as tolerance and supporters reading it as caution.

WHAT HAPPENS NEXT

The immediate outcome is a partial retreat. The most contested images are gone, while the exhibition itself remains open under the culture ministry's sponsorship. The dispute echoes earlier debates over public memory. The culture ministry has not said whether it will review the remaining material or leave the rest of the display untouched.

That compromise is unlikely to end the argument. Opponents want a fuller reckoning with the groups that shaped Rauti's career and with the violence connected to them. The dispute is likely to return when the exhibition closes, as opponents demand a formal account of how it was approved.

Brothers of Italy faces a familiar balancing act. It wants to honour figures from the post-war right while maintaining distance from fascist symbolism. The party's base includes voters who identify with the post-war right, while its governing partners expect a clear distance from fascist symbols.

The episode may become a test of how the party handles historical memory in government. Statements from Meloni will matter more than those of junior ministers. A statement from the prime minister would settle the question of where the government stands, and its absence keeps the argument alive.

The broader consequence is a renewed debate over how Italy displays the sources of its modern extreme right, a question that museums and parliaments will keep facing. Other European countries have faced similar disputes over memorials and exhibitions, and Italy's handling of this one will be watched closely.
