# The Fed's October Decision Rests on Two Series Moving in Opposite Directions

**Label:** Research Note
**Section:** Macro — Monetary Policy
**Published:** 16 October 2026
**Prepared by:** Y-Square

---

The Federal Open Market Committee's October meeting is a coin-flip in market pricing, and the reason is that the two data series carrying the most weight for the decision are now pointing in opposite directions.

## What the data show

September consumer prices rose 3.7% year over year and 0.6% month over month; the core index, which excludes food and energy, rose 2.6% year over year and 0.3% month over month (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Consumer Price Index, September 2026, as reported by Reuters, 14 October 2026). Both readings came in above consensus. The Committee's target is 2%.

Against that, September nonfarm payrolls added 29,000 jobs and the unemployment rate rose to 4.2% (BLS, Employment Situation, September 2026, as reported by Business Standard, October 2026). That is a weak labour print by any recent standard.

The Committee last acted on 16 September 2026, raising the target range 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00% on a 12–0 vote, its first increase in three years (Federal Reserve, FOMC statement, 16 September 2026). Core PCE, the Committee's preferred inflation gauge, stood at 3.34% year over year in July 2026 (FRED series PCEPILFE). The ten-year Treasury yield was 5.24% on 28 September 2026 (FRED series DGS10).

## What the market now prices

Interest-rate futures implied roughly even odds of a 25 basis point increase at the October meeting after the CPI release, against approximately 20% before it (CME Group FedWatch, 14 October 2026). Pricing for at least one increase by the December meeting ran above 50% (same source). The Committee meets 27–28 October and 8–9 December 2026 (Federal Reserve, meeting calendar). Secondary reporting of the calendar is inconsistent — some outlets list 28–29 October — and the Federal Reserve's own published calendar is the one to cite.

## Why the two series cannot both be decisive

An inflation print above target argues for tightening. A payroll print of 29,000 with unemployment rising argues for patience. The Committee cannot treat both as binding without taking a position on which one it is willing to be wrong about. That is the whole of the October question; the rest is commentary.

The election calendar is a factor the Committee does not control and will not cite. The meeting falls six days before the US midterm elections (Federal Reserve calendar; US election calendar). Chair Kevin Warsh, asked directly about the rate path, said on 10 October that he does not "prejudge meetings" (Business Standard, reporting a fireside chat with IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva, Bangkok). That is the only forward guidance the Committee has given, and it is a refusal to give any.

## Where the data are thin

- The September payroll figure is a first estimate and is routinely revised. June and July 2026 payrolls were revised up by a combined 55,000 in the August release (BLS, Employment Situation, August 2026, released 4 September 2026, USDL-26-1435). Treat 29,000 as provisional.
- The market-implied odds are a snapshot, not a forecast, and moved more than 20 percentage points within 24 hours of the CPI release. Quote them with a timestamp or not at all.
- The causal link between the September CPI upside and energy prices is asserted in secondary reporting, not established here. The price series are primary and citable; the attribution is not.
- The claim that an October increase would be "politically fraught" is commentary from named economists, not a fact about the Committee's behaviour.

## What would falsify this read

The read here is that the October outcome is genuinely undetermined by the data available today. It would be wrong if either of two observations appears:

1. **Core CPI below 2.5% year over year for two consecutive months.** That would mean the September upside was noise and the easing case holds without further tightening.
2. **An October FOMC statement that drops the "inflation remains elevated" language used in September.** The Committee's own words are the cleanest signal available; a change in that sentence would resolve the question the data leave open.

Conversely, a second increase would confirm the inflation reading as the binding constraint, with the ten-year yield above 5.5% and the curve bear-flattening as the market's response.

## What to watch

The October FOMC statement language on inflation, released with the decision on 28 October 2026, and the next core CPI print. Either resolves the read; nothing else on the calendar between now and then will.
