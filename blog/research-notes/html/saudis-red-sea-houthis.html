A. Contextual Summary (Source-Accurate)

Saudi Arabia is preparing an offensive against Iran-backed Houthi militants in Yemen, according to six people with knowledge of the plans. The operation is expected to be launched in the coming weeks and would be led on the ground by Yemeni forces overseen by Riyadh, with Saudi air support. The United States is providing intelligence and targeting support; regional and European partners are contributing mainly defensive aid and are not expected to take direct combat roles.

The immediate trigger is Houthi gains along the Yemeni coast and their seizure of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait last month, which gives them significant control over a critical shipping chokepoint. Houthis also launched missile and drone attacks on Saudi shipping and oil infrastructure as a spillover from the wider Iran conflict.

Saudi officials are considering two options: a narrowly focused assault around Bab el-Mandeb, or a broader multi-front offensive targeting additional governorates (Al-Bayda, Marib, Taiz, Al-Jawf). More than 100,000 Yemeni troops could be mobilized depending on scale.

Bab el-Mandeb's importance has grown because the Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed by the Iran conflict, making the Red Sea route a key alternative between the Mediterranean and Asia. Riyadh has concluded it cannot resume peace talks with the Houthis while they retain control of Bab el-Mandeb.

Timing estimates range from within a week to after the US midterm elections in early November. A former US ambassador to Saudi Arabia expects any operation to be modest in scope, focused on retaking coastal areas to restore maritime traffic rather than a large-scale campaign. Saudi concerns include protecting oil facilities and infrastructure from Houthi drone and missile attacks; allies including Pakistan and Turkey have reportedly provided military equipment and advisers.

B. Political Landscape (Y-Square Analysis)

This is a regional power play masquerading as a local counter-offensive. The Saudi-Houthi conflict, rooted in Yemen's civil war since 2015, has been subsumed into the Iran war's wider arc. Iran's strategic leverage in the Red Sea now runs through the Houthis, giving Tehran an indirect chokehold on a second maritime passage beyond Hormuz.

For the Gulf, the message is narrow: no negotiated settlement while Bab el-Mandeb is in Houthi hands. For Washington, the calculus is constrained - intelligence support without direct combat - reflecting both US overstretch in the Middle East and the political timing of the US midterm calendar. The Saudi reliance on Pakistani troops already stationed in the kingdom, and on Turkish drones, signals Riyadh is assembling a coalition of convenience rather than awaiting Western permission.

Domestically, the offensive is a test of Saudi leadership cohesion under Prince Mohammed bin Salman: a failed or prolonged operation risks embarrassing Riyadh and handing the Houthis a propaganda victory, while a limited, successful push would restore the pre-September status quo and strengthen Saudi diplomatic standing.

C. Geo-Economic Implications (Y-Square Analysis)

Bab el-Mandeb is now the pressure valve for global shipping after Hormuz's closure. If Houthi control persists, vessels rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope face longer voyages, higher freight costs, and tighter delivery schedules - all of which pass through to energy, food, and manufactured goods prices even in the absence of new conflict.

Saudi objectives are therefore as commercial as military: reopening the Red Sea to normal traffic to protect oil exports, stabilize maritime insurance premiums, and preserve Yanbu as a functioning Red Sea port. The French decision to send defensive systems and troops to Yanbu underscores that port's value as an energy outlet.

A contained Saudi operation that retakes coastal areas could restore the status quo and ease shipping costs. A broader or failed campaign risks more Houthi retaliatory strikes on ports and infrastructure, extending the disruption and potentially pushing insurers and shippers to further reroute.

D. Macro-Economic Reading (Y-Square Analysis)

The macro channel here is an energy-and-shipping risk premium, not a data print. The source contains no hard macroeconomic indicators, so no statistics are claimed beyond what Reuters reported.

The transmission runs through three routes:

1. Energy prices - any disruption to Red Sea traffic or Saudi oil infrastructure feeds directly into crude price expectations, with pass-through to transport and fertilizer costs.

2. Trade costs - longer detours and higher insurance raise landed costs for Asian and European importers, pressuring goods inflation on both continents.

3. Monetary spillover - central banks facing already-elevated inflation from energy shocks may delay rate cuts if shipping disruption worsens.

Net assessment: a credible, limited Saudi offensive would likely reduce the war-risk premium by restoring maritime normalcy. Conversely, escalation extends the inflation impulse. The key variable to watch is whether the Bab el-Mandeb returns to secure passage, not the intensity of Saudi strikes.
