Canada Builds Trade Options Beyond Washington

Mark Carney says a US trade agreement remains possible but complex, while Canada expands links with China, ASEAN, the Philippines and India.

By Y-Squre Research/2026-11-19/…

Key points

  • Mark Carney said a trade agreement with the United States remains possible but is more complex than recent talks with China and other partners.
  • Canada has reached agreements with ASEAN and the Philippines and expects to sign a trade deal with India in the coming months.
  • Carney said talks with China had reduced tariffs and resolved more than 90 per cent of trade irritants.
  • Carney and Donald Trump held a meeting lasting just under 20 minutes in Shenzhen, according to Carney’s office.
  • Canada’s trade dispute with the United States followed the collapse of formal talks in August and reciprocal tariffs.

Canada's trade relationship with the United States has been strained by tariffs and President Donald Trump's calls for Canada to become the 51st state. The report says the dispute worsened after Mark Carney's government withdrew from formal talks in August and both sides accused the other of changing terms late. The dispute has therefore become a question of market access.

After those talks collapsed, Washington imposed tariffs on billions of dollars of Canadian goods. Ottawa responded with tariffs on American products, turning a difficult negotiation into a wider dispute affecting trade between two closely connected neighbours. The response made the disagreement costly for exporters on both sides.

Canada's relationship with China has its own difficult history. The report traces a severe breakdown to China's detention of two Canadians after Canada arrested a Chinese telecom executive at the request of the United States, alongside Canadian accusations of interference in federal elections. The earlier episode remains a constraint on renewed cooperation.

Carney later began rebuilding ties with Beijing through meetings with Xi Jinping, first at an APEC gathering in South Korea a year earlier and then in Beijing in January. The report says those contacts continued with a further meeting in Shenzhen during the current APEC summit. Those meetings provide a sequence rather than a single reset.

These parallel histories help explain Ottawa's search for alternatives. Carney argues that relying too heavily on one trading partner creates risk, while critics in Canada say the United States remains the country's more significant relationship because of longstanding ties and geography. Diversification is presented as insurance, not a complete replacement.

Carney says a strong trade arrangement with Washington remains possible, but the negotiations are more complicated than Canada's recent discussions with China and other partners. After meeting Trump in Shenzhen, he described their exchange as constructive but said a US agreement was not close to completion. Carney's wording leaves room for further talks with Washington.

He pointed to recent agreements with ASEAN and the Philippines, and said Canada expected to sign a trade deal with India in the coming months. His message was that Canada would continue developing options rather than wait for a US decision. The other deals strengthen Canada's options, but their impact remains unmeasured.

Carney said talks with China had reduced tariffs and resolved more than 90 per cent of trade irritants. The report identifies affected goods including Canadian canola, seafood and pork, as well as Chinese steel, aluminium and electric vehicles. The distinction is between a possible agreement and a finished one.

The meeting with Trump lasted just under 20 minutes, according to Carney's office, and included senior officials from both governments. The official readout said they discussed work towards an agreement intended to support jobs, affordability and Canada's sovereignty. The tariff reductions are specific progress, not a full settlement.

Foreign Affairs Minister Anita Anand described continued contact between the leaders as useful. Conservative trade critic Stephanie Kusie welcomed diversification in principle but argued that closer engagement with China should not distract from the failure to secure a US deal. The meeting's short duration does not establish its final impact.

Diversification could strengthen Canada's negotiating position if new agreements create credible alternatives for exporters. The more markets Canadian firms can use, the less pressure Ottawa may feel to accept terms it considers unfavourable in a single negotiation. Anand's view and Kusie's criticism show competing domestic assessments.

That strategy will take time to become a durable substitute for trade with the United States. The report describes new agreements and negotiations, but it does not quantify how much trade can shift to China, ASEAN, the Philippines or India in the near term. The immediate issue is whether talks can produce enforceable terms.

There are also constraints on how far Canada can deepen ties with Beijing. Kusie's criticism reflects concerns about China's human-rights record and previous tariffs, while Carney says Canada is focusing on commodities rather than sensitive advanced technologies. Alternative buyers matter only if exporters can use those routes.

The US relationship may still improve because both governments have said discussions will continue. A mutually acceptable arrangement remains possible under the existing continental framework or a revised trade policy, although Carney says important differences remain unresolved. A larger network would take time to develop commercial depth.

The strongest interpretation is not that Canada is replacing the United States, but that it is trying to reduce dependence on any single market. Whether that becomes meaningful will depend on implementation, commercial demand and the ability to sustain agreements through future disputes. The source records concerns, not a settled judgment about China.

The most likely outcome is a broader set of trade relationships alongside continued efforts to reach an agreement with Washington. Carney's comments indicate that Canada intends to keep both tracks open rather than choose one partner and abandon the other. Existing continental ties remain a strong reason to keep negotiating.

In the near term, the ASEAN, Philippines and China arrangements give Ottawa additional channels to discuss market access and resolve specific barriers. The expected India agreement could add another option, although its timing and final terms remain uncertain. That approach reduces exposure without removing the original relationship.

At the same time, the United States is likely to remain central to Canada's trade planning. The report describes an existing continental agreement, dense commercial ties and ongoing direct contact between Carney and Trump, all of which make a complete pivot unlikely. The article gives no trade-flow figures to measure the shift.

This path depends on the new arrangements becoming practical routes for exporters, not just diplomatic announcements. It also requires Canadian officials to manage concerns about China while preserving enough trust with Washington to continue negotiations. Officials must balance commercial openings with public concerns.

The clearest measure of progress will be whether trade flows and unresolved barriers actually change over time. Until then, Carney's approach is best understood as risk reduction: building additional options while leaving the door open to a US deal.
