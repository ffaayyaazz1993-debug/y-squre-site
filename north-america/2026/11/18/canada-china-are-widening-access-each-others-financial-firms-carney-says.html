Canada and China open financial channels in Shenzhen

Canada and China are widening access for each other's financial firms, renewing a currency swap and cutting Ottawa's reliance on the United States market.

By Y-Squre Research / 2026-11-18 / …

Key points

  • Canada and China are widening access for each other's financial firms and have renewed a central-bank currency swap.
  • Canadian exports to China have risen by more than a third this year, with two-way trade near C$125 billion.
  • China will extend tariff relief on about C$400 million of Canadian goods; Canada will keep steel and aluminium remissions for two more years.
  • Carney frames the shift as cutting reliance on the United States, not replacing it as Canada's largest partner.

A Renewed Channel across the Pacific

Canada and China have spent several years rebuilding a relationship that had grown distant, and this week marked the clearest step yet. Years of strained relations had cooled trade and investment between the two countries, leaving a cautious business mood on both sides. Prime Minister Mark Carney has been pressing for a reset since he took office last year.

The pivot reflects a wider calculation in Ottawa about how far an economy can lean on a single partner. Successive American administrations have imposed hefty tariffs on a range of Canadian products. That external pressure gave the push toward Asia a practical edge as well as a strategic one, and it is unlikely to fade quickly.

Foreign Minister Anita Anand has described the approach as step-by-step rather than a sudden embrace. The aim is to re-engage with the world's second-largest economy without discarding longstanding ties. Officials present the shift as diversification, not replacement, and they point to the scale of existing commerce.

The setting mattered. Carney spoke at the 2026 APEC CEO Summit in Shenzhen, just ahead of the APEC China 2026 gathering in the southern Guangdong tech hub. That venue placed the bilateral reset alongside a wider regional conversation about supply chains and market access, the kind of development our North America coverage tracks closely.

Trade between the two countries already stands at about C$125 billion, or $87 billion. Canadian exports to China have risen by more than a third so far this year. That growth gives the latest measures a real base to build on, rather than a starting point of zero.

What Shenzhen Produced This Week

Carney said Canada and China are widening access for financial firms in each other's markets. He described the change as giving both sides tools to act decisively during times of financial stress. The Wednesday announcement in Shenzhen framed the move as practical preparation rather than a symbolic gesture.

The two central banks have renewed a currency swap arrangement, and the countries are now issuing each other's currencies. Carney linked this directly to resilience. When businesses depend on too few currencies or a single payment channel, he said, disruption can spread quickly once it arrives.

The financial opening sits alongside a tariff arrangement announced the previous day. President Xi Jinping and Carney agreed to extend and expand a preliminary deal giving Canadian farmers and seafood harvesters better access to China. A statement from Carney's office set out the specific terms.

China will provide additional tariff relief on about C$400 million, or $280 million, of Canadian exports. Frozen pork cuts and fish and seafood feature prominently in that figure. In return, Canada will extend by two years existing remission measures for certain Chinese steel and aluminium products due to expire at the end of 2026.

Carney also said Canada has restored market access for more than 99% of the trade previously hit by punitive tariff action. He told reporters the American trading relationship is much larger and more complex. The United States changed its trade objectives, he said, and Ottawa is focused on getting the right deal for Canadians.

Whether the Reset Holds

Whether these steps produce lasting change depends on forces outside either capital. Financial access and currency lines are far easier to announce than to embed in daily practice. Their real value shows up only when a genuine shock tests them.

There are reasons to think the momentum could persist. Both governments have put names, dates and volumes to the commitments, and the tariff relief is already scheduled to take effect. Xi noted that exchanges and cooperation have fully resumed since a January pact meant to advance the China-Canada strategic partnership.

A standing currency swap gives central banks a channel that does not need to be rebuilt from scratch during a crisis. Issuing each other's currencies adds a second layer of flexibility. Few such arrangements are ever tested in calm conditions, which makes their true depth difficult to judge from the outside.

There are equally good reasons for caution. Trade contacts that changed together can change back, especially if tariff pressure from the United States were to ease. The American market remains far larger than the Chinese one, and Canadian exporters will not turn away from it quickly.

Co-occurrence is not the same as cause. The warmer ties and the tariff relief arrived in the same week, but that alone does not prove one produced the other. The genuine test is whether companies actually use the new channels when conditions tighten and conventional routes become expensive.

A Narrower, Firmer Partnership

If the consequences do materialise, the most likely outcome is a narrower but firmer partnership rather than a sweeping realignment. The measures are practical and sector-specific by design, targeting payment plumbing, pork, seafood, steel and aluminium. That makes the deal easier to defend at home but smaller in scope.

Canadian producers would gain a more reliable route into a large market just as American tariffs bite. Chinese steel and aluminium makers would keep favourable treatment for two more years. Both sides would receive something concrete without signing a broad new treaty.

For the financial sector, the swap line and mutual market access could lower the cost of settling trade in each other's currencies. That would reduce reliance on a single payment route. Whether banks pass that benefit on to clients is a separate and open question.

The overall trade figure would probably grow modestly rather than leap. Two-way commerce of C$125 billion is already substantial, and a third more exports would be a meaningful gain. It would not amount to replacing the United States as Canada's dominant economic partner any time soon.

The condition attached to all of this is continued American pressure. If Washington softens its tariff stance, the urgency behind Ottawa's push toward Asia could fade. If it hardens instead, the channels opened in Shenzhen may prove more valuable than they first appeared.
