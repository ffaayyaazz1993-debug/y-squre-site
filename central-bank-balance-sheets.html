The balance sheet is the policy: reading central banks past the rate decision

Markets price the rate decision, then react to what the central bank actually did to its portfolio. Those are not the same event, and the gap between them is where most of the mispricing sits.

Y-Square Research · 26 September 2026 · 8 min read

When a central bank changes its policy rate, the statement, the dot plot and the press conference absorb nearly all the attention. That is reasonable — the rate is the cleanest, fastest-moving expression of policy. But it is not the only expression, and for most major central banks it is no longer the dominant one.

On the liability side of a balance sheet sit reserves and, increasingly, interest-bearing deposits: obligations the bank owes the banking system and the public. Those follow the policy rate mechanically. On the asset side sit securities the bank bought outright — government bonds, agency debt, and in some cases corporate paper and exchange-traded funds. That side is where intention lives, because it changes only when someone decides to change it.

Why the asset side leads

Asset purchases and sales are slow, large and deliberate. A rate cut can be reversed at the next meeting; a balance sheet that has grown by fifteen percent of GDP is not unwound by a single decision. That inertia is what makes the asset side informative: it reveals where a committee has concluded it wants to be, expressed in a form that survives changes of mind about the near-term rate path.

The practical implication is a timing lead. Our reading of post-2008 and post-2020 policy sequences is that meaningful changes in the asset side have preceded comparable changes in the stance of the policy rate by somewhere between two and three quarters. That is a rough estimate from a small number of episodes, not a measured constant, and it should be treated as a prior to test rather than a rule to apply.

What to read, in order

Three series carry most of the signal, and all three are published by the central bank itself or by a statistical agency — not by a bank’s commentary.

  • Holding-period composition. The share of the portfolio in bills versus coupons. A rising bill share signals a flatter, more defensive posture; a falling share signals duration being taken on. This moves first and moves most clearly.
  • Reinvestment and runoff rates. The monthly caps on what is allowed to mature without replacement. These are published in advance, which makes them the one part of the asset side that is genuinely forecastable rather than inferred.
  • Total size relative to trend inflation. The crudest measure, and the one most often quoted out of context. Only interpretable alongside the first two.

Read the series in that order. Composition leads, runoff caps confirm, and the headline size is a summary rather than a signal.

Where this framework fails

It fails in a specific way: during a funding crisis, when a central bank expands its balance sheet to absorb a shock in a market it does not control, and the expansion carries no information about intended policy. Any framework that reads size as intent will misread that episode badly. Distinguishing a policy action from a plumbing action is, in our view, the hardest part of the work and the part most worth getting wrong carefully.

It also degrades in small open economies, where the domestic central bank’s balance sheet is heavily influenced by foreign holdings of domestic currency. The asset side stops being a clean read on domestic intent.

The position this implies

For asset allocation, the useful output is not a forecast of the next rate decision. It is a prior on the shape of the policy path — how much duration the committee is willing to add, how quickly, and what it would take to reverse course. Where the asset side has been expanding while the market prices a shallow rate path, the gap is where the return is; where the asset side is contracting while the market prices aggressive easing, it is where the loss is.

Neither of those gaps is a certainty. They are observations about where to look harder.

Method note: this note relies on publicly published balance sheet and holdings data from central banks and national statistical agencies. No proprietary or non-public data was used. Views are the author’s and are subject to change.
