Xi Jinping Opens APEC with a Free-Trade Appeal

Xi Jinping used the APEC CEO Summit in Shenzhen to call for free and open trade, while new US chip curbs and a planned three-way meeting raised the stakes.

By Y-Squre Research / 2026-11-18 / …

Key points

  • Xi opened the APEC CEO Summit in Shenzhen with a call for free and open trade and a warning against protectionism.
  • The speech followed new US restrictions on China's access to advanced AI chips from companies such as Nvidia; China has pledged to retaliate.
  • Trump, Xi and Putin are set for a three-way meeting on Thursday covering Iran, Ukraine, trade, AI and nuclear weapons.
  • Smaller powers fear great-power deals could disregard their security interests; Taiwan and Japan flagged Russian aircraft incursions.
  • The most likely outcome is modest deliverables and a carefully worded communique rather than a broad settlement.

Chinese President Xi Jinping opened the APEC CEO Summit in Shenzhen on Wednesday with a call for "free and open trade" and a warning against protectionism. The speech came as Washington prepared further restrictions on China's access to advanced AI chips, and as President Donald Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin headed toward a high-stakes three-way meeting on Thursday. Xi used the business audience to set a contrast: Beijing would lower tariffs and open markets, while Washington tightened export controls.

Shenzhen is China's technology hub, and APEC gathers economies that account for a large share of global trade. Xi told executives the region should uphold a rules-based trading system with the WTO at its core. He posed rhetorical questions about how the Asia Pacific should respond to a changing world, framing China as the defender of openness.

Western partners criticise China for trade surpluses above $1 trillion and industrial policies that support manufacturing at the expense of consumers. Beijing says its growth model suits a catching-up economy. Washington and Brussels worry that supply-chain dominance gives China leverage and threatens their industries and jobs. Xi said China would keep investing in its industrial complex while developing technologies that benefit others.

The week extends beyond the summit. Trump concluded meetings in Manila, and Putin landed after Ukraine destroyed the Kerch bridge linking Crimea to Russia. Thursday's three-way meeting will cover Iran and Ukraine, plus trade, AI and nuclear weapons. Smaller powers fear great-power deals could disregard their security. See the Manila summit week.

APEC is a useful stage because it gathers developed and developing economies under one banner. That makes it harder for any single government to frame the trade agenda alone, and gives smaller members a chance to press for protective language.

Shenzhen Summit Opens Against Chip Curbs

Xi's remarks followed new US restrictions on China's access to advanced AI chips from companies such as Nvidia. China has pledged to retaliate, so the trade truce is entering another stressful phase. The timing was deliberate: Xi needed a platform before meeting Trump and Putin, and APEC gave him a business audience.

He said the region should maintain stable global industrial and supply chains. He pledged to lower tariffs, open markets further, and stand against protectionism and unilateralism. Each promise can be read as both an offer and a defence of the existing model.

George Chen of The Asia Group said the message would resonate with Global South countries. He argued that it reinforces Beijing's effort to contrast its technology strategy with Washington's. The contrast is the week's central diplomatic play.

The summit sits inside wider regional anxieties. Taiwan and Japan flagged Russian aircraft incursions on Tuesday. Trump floated a possible meeting with Kim Jong Un. The three-way meeting has drawn Yalta comparisons, shorthand for great-power deals imposed on smaller countries.

Those comparisons are not evidence of what the meeting will produce. They do show how smaller powers fear being sidelined. The week's diplomacy will be judged by whether the communique protects their interests or merely records the great powers' preferences.

What Xi Said and What Beijing Wants

Xi told the audience that the region must uphold the rules-based multilateral trading system with the WTO at its core. He called for free and open trade and investment, and for stable supply chains. The language is familiar, but the Shenzhen audience gave it a sharper edge because the US had just moved to restrict advanced chips.

Beijing's immediate objective is to slow escalation. By promising tariff reductions and market opening, Xi can argue China is willing to make concessions while Washington tightens controls. That argument is useful in capitals wary of choosing between the two economies. It also gives China room to continue industrial policies Western governments call excess capacity.

The second objective is to stabilise the relationship before the fourth Trump-Xi meeting this year. Most analysts expect ties to remain relatively stable, with a G20 gathering in Miami scheduled for December. A working lunch Thursday, then a three-way session with Putin, offers Trump a chance to claim progress. For Xi, the value is showing China can manage the relationship without abandoning its technology ambitions.

The third objective is to reassure regional governments that China will not be pushed into a corner by the chip curbs. The promise that new technologies will benefit other countries is aimed at that audience. Belief depends on what follows: tariff reductions, licences for foreign firms, and restraint in retaliation. Until then, the speech is positioning rather than policy.

Will the Trade Truce Survive the Talks?

The truce is fragile, and the chip restrictions are the clearest test. China has pledged to retaliate, so controls and countermeasures could resume even as the leaders meet. The summit gives both sides a chance to signal restraint, but the underlying technology competition is intact. A meeting can lower the temperature without removing the cause of the fever.

Several drivers could still make the talks consequential. Institutional pressure in Washington is building around export controls, and Trump's electoral position gives him an incentive to secure visible outcomes. Economic incentives run both ways because the two economies remain linked through supply chains. Managed rivalries tend to produce narrow deals rather than broad settlements.

There are equally strong reasons the talks might fizzle. Congress may push for faster restrictions regardless of the summit. Chinese retaliation could target the same advanced-chip sector, raising the cost of understanding. Smaller countries may resist a binary choice, and the security agenda could crowd out trade. Co-occurrence of a summit and a truce does not establish that one sustains the other.

The Most Likely Outcome for the Region

The most probable result is modest, concrete deliverables rather than a sweeping arrangement. Expect restated support for APEC openness, language on supply-chain stability, and a trilateral communique that avoids firm promises on Ukraine or Iran. Trump and Xi can point to a working lunch and a planned fourth meeting this year as evidence the relationship remains manageable.

That depends on one condition: neither the chip case nor regional incidents escalate during the summit. If export-control legislation advances in Washington while talks are underway, truce language becomes harder to sustain. If the Kerch bridge attack or Russian aircraft incursions produce a sharper response, the three-way meeting becomes more confrontational and the trade file is pushed aside.

For the region, the risk is being sidelined by great-power deals. Smaller powers in Europe and Asia fear agreements that disregard their security interests, and the Yalta comparison captures that anxiety. The most possible consequence is managed rivalry with narrow, transactional deals rather than a broad settlement.

APEC economies will hedge between Washington and Beijing. The signal to watch is whether China follows rhetoric with tariff reductions and market opening, and whether Washington pauses further chip curbs. If both hold, the truce stabilises; if neither holds, the summit marks a move toward open contest over technology and trade.
