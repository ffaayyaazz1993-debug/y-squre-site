Mexico and US Trade Talks Move Closer, Sheinbaum Says

Claudia Sheinbaum says talks with Washington are far along after an APEC meeting with Donald Trump, with tariffs and border pressures central.

By Y-Squre Research/2026/11/18/…

Key points

  • Sheinbaum called her APEC meeting with Trump very good.
  • She said bilateral trade negotiations are very far along.
  • Mexico wants relief from US tariffs on steel, aluminium and autos.
  • The two leaders will meet again at the G20 in Miami in December.

THE APEC MEETING AND ITS READOUT

Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum said she had a very good meeting with Donald Trump on the sidelines of the APEC conference in Shenzhen. She described progress on a shared agenda without releasing a detailed outcome. The two leaders spoke for about half an hour, according to officials familiar with the meeting, though no joint statement was issued afterward.

In a post on X, she wrote that the two countries continue to make progress. At a press conference, she said Trump had been briefed by Mexico's economy secretary and was in a very good mood. Her tone was notably warmer than after previous encounters, when tariff threats had dominated the public exchange between the two governments.

She said the negotiations were very far along, but gave no timetable or list of agreed measures. She added that she would meet Trump again at the G20 meeting in Miami in December. The absence of specifics leaves room for both sides to claim progress while the technical teams continue working through the disputed tariff lines.

The statement was brief but consequential. Mexico and the United States are each other's largest trading partners, and the outcome affects factories and workers on both sides of the border. Any change in tariff treatment would ripple through factories in both countries, where components cross the border multiple times during production.

The meeting came after Washington declined in July to extend the US-Mexico-Canada agreement for another sixteen-year term, pushing the issues into a bilateral track. That decision left the agreement in a review cycle rather than a long-term renewal, raising uncertainty for investors planning new capacity.

WHAT IS AT STAKE IN THE TRADE TALKS

The two countries exchanged about 779 billion dollars in goods in the first nine months of this year. Auto supply chains routinely cross the border several times before a vehicle is finished. The figure underscores how deeply the two economies are integrated, and why even modest tariff changes can shift production decisions quickly.

Mexico's immediate priority is relief from US Section 232 tariffs on steel, aluminium and cars. Around 80 percent of Mexican exports already enter the United States without tariffs. Those tariffs were imposed on national security grounds, which makes them harder to negotiate away than ordinary trade remedies.

That figure explains the urgency. A relatively small change in tariff treatment can reshape investment decisions across the manufacturing belt and alter hiring plans. A change in treatment for the remaining fifth of exports would be enough to alter the economics of several major assembly plants.

Sheinbaum has also sought to reassure Washington that closer ties with China will not weaken the relationship with the United States. The concern in Washington is that Chinese components could enter the US market through Mexico with lower duties than direct imports would face.

At the same time, Mexico has been tightening checks on Asian goods that US officials fear could reach the American market through Mexican territory, a pattern noted in earlier trade coverage. Mexican customs officials have increased inspections of suspicious shipments, a step intended to show Washington that the backdoor concern is being taken seriously.

THE TARIFF AND BORDER PRESSURES

Trump threatened last week to double tariffs on Mexico if it failed to stop migrant caravans. Sheinbaum said the caravan behind the threat remained far south in Chiapas. The threat linked two unrelated issues in a single sentence, a tactic that has become familiar in the administration's approach to trade negotiations.

Officials said the group had shrunk to 781 people and were urging migrants to return home. The dispute shows how trade and migration are increasingly linked in negotiations. Mexican officials say the group is moving slowly and that most participants are seeking work rather than intending to cross the border.

For manufacturers, that linkage creates uncertainty. A tariff decision can be shaped by events far from a factory gate and by statements made during election campaigns. A factory planning a new line cannot easily factor in a tariff decision that may depend on events hundreds of miles away.

Mexico's challenge is to separate the issues enough to win relief on tariffs while still cooperating on border enforcement. That is harder than it sounds when both leaders face domestic pressure. The two governments have cooperated on enforcement before, but trust has been strained by repeated public threats from Washington.

The two leaders have reason to strike a deal. US firms rely on Mexican suppliers, and Mexican exporters rely on access to the US market more than any single tariff line. That mutual dependence is the strongest argument for a deal, and both sides know it even when public rhetoric suggests otherwise.

LIKELY NEXT STEPS

The most likely outcome is a narrow package of tariff adjustments rather than a sweeping agreement. Both sides can claim progress without resolving every dispute. A narrow package could cover specific tariff lines while leaving the broader agreement review for a later round of talks.

Sheinbaum's very far along language suggests technical work is advanced, but formal approval remains necessary in both capitals before anything is announced. Legislators in both countries will want to see the details before endorsing anything, and election-year pressures could slow the process.

The G20 meeting in Miami offers a natural deadline. A declaration there would give both leaders a visible win and a chance to frame the deal as practical cooperation. The summit setting also gives both leaders a stage to present the outcome as evidence that personal diplomacy can deliver results.

The risk is that a new border incident derails the timetable. Trump has shown he is willing to use tariff threats as leverage when other pressures build. A single incident at the border could undo weeks of technical work, which is why both teams are watching the caravan situation closely.

For businesses, the practical signal is to prepare for incremental change rather than a clean reset. Supply chains will keep adapting while the talks continue. Companies are already diversifying suppliers and holding extra inventory as a hedge against sudden changes in tariff treatment.
