Saudi Arabia Gears Up for a Long Yemen Campaign

Riyadh is preparing for Houthi operations to extend into 2027, betting on territorial gains and outside support before any talks.

By Y-Squre Research/2026/11/18/…

Key points

  • Saudi officials expect the Yemen campaign to last up to six more months.
  • Houthi forces have taken the Red Sea coast and the Bab el-Mandeb strait.
  • Riyadh believes US offensive support could speed up gains on the ground.
  • A wider Iran war deal could allow the Yemen front to wind down.

WHY RIYADH PLANS FOR A LONGER CAMPAIGN

Saudi Arabia is preparing for its campaign against Yemen's Houthi movement to continue well into next year, according to two Western officials briefed by Saudi counterparts. They say Riyadh now expects up to six more months of fighting. The officials spoke on condition of anonymity because the discussions were private, and their accounts were consistent on the expected duration of the campaign.

The plan reflects a decision to seek territorial gains before entering any negotiation with the Houthis. Officials familiar with the discussions say Saudi leaders want their Yemeni allies to hold stronger positions first. That sequencing matters: Riyadh wants to negotiate from a position of strength rather than accept a pause that freezes the current front lines.

A third source confirmed the broad assessment. All three said a settlement in the wider Iran war could allow the Yemen front to wind down more quickly than the current plan suggests. The connection between the two conflicts is the central variable in every assessment of how long the fighting in Yemen will continue.

Saudi officials have publicly acknowledged the war's costs more openly than in the past. The conflict has displaced hundreds of thousands and disrupted daily life inside the kingdom. Public events have been postponed, and the kingdom's investment drive has lost momentum as the war drags on without a clear end.

The Saudi Foreign Ministry declined to comment on the officials' account. The absence of a public response leaves the planning details attributed to people speaking without names. The silence leaves open the question of whether the six-month estimate is a plan or a worst-case assumption shared privately with allies.

BATTLEFIELD REVERSALS AND THE BAB EL-MANDEB

The renewed fighting began after a ceasefire that had held since 2022 broke down this summer. Since then, Houthi forces have taken control of much of the Red Sea coast and the strategic Bab el-Mandeb strait. Control of the strait gives the Houthis leverage over one of the world's most important shipping chokepoints, far beyond the battlefield itself.

They have also encircled Taiz, a city in the highlands above Aden, the temporary base of Yemen's internationally recognised government. The encirclement has complicated efforts to protect the southern coast. Taiz has been under pressure for years, and its population has endured shortages of water, medicine and fuel throughout the conflict.

Yemeni government forces mounted a counterattack last month and reclaimed territory near the strait. They then withdrew over the past weekend to reinforce Aden, a significant reverse for the Saudi-backed side. The withdrawal was described by officials as a necessary choice to avoid losing the temporary capital entirely.

Saudi and Yemeni commanders are regrouping. One official said they may shift the focus to eastern fronts where lines have held or where local allies have made limited advances. Eastern fronts offer more open terrain, where armored units and air support can be used more effectively than in the coastal mountains.

The pattern suggests a war of position rather than a quick victory. Each side is trying to improve its bargaining terrain before any pause takes hold, as recent reporting has shown. Neither side can currently claim the momentum needed to force the other into talks on its own terms.

THE WIDER IRAN WAR AND OUTSIDE SUPPORT

Yemen has become the most active front in the wider Iran war, linking local grievances to regional rivalry. The Houthis are aligned with Tehran, while Saudi Arabia leads a coalition backing the Yemeni government. The alignment means that decisions taken in Tehran about the wider war ripple directly into the calculations of commanders in Yemen.

Riyadh believes American offensive support could change the balance. Officials say Washington has so far withheld that support, but a change could allow gains within weeks rather than months. Without that support, Saudi planners must assume that any gains will come slowly and at a higher cost in equipment and personnel.

The Trump administration has not publicly committed to direct operations in Yemen. That leaves Saudi planners preparing for a long campaign without assuming fresh US firepower. The administration's restraint reflects a broader reluctance to open another front while the Iran war continues to consume attention and resources.

Energy markets watch the strait closely because it sits near a route used for global shipping. Attacks on tankers have already raised insurance costs and complicated deliveries across the region. Insurers have raised premiums for vessels transiting the area, and some operators are rerouting cargoes around the Cape of Good Hope.

The wider diplomacy is moving in parallel. Oman and Qatar have hosted talks on maritime corridors, while Saudi officials insist any drawdown should follow battlefield progress. Those talks have produced no public agreement so far, but they keep a channel open between the parties most directly affected.

WHAT A PROLONGED CAMPAIGN COULD PRODUCE

The most likely path is a prolonged stalemate punctuated by bursts of intense fighting. Neither side appears ready to concede, and outside backers can keep the conflict supplied for months. Supplies of weapons and fuel continue to reach both sides, which removes the pressure that a shortage would otherwise create.

Saudi planners may get the territorial gains they seek, but those gains are unlikely to produce a decisive settlement. The Houthis have shown an ability to absorb losses and counterattack. Past rounds of fighting have shown that territorial changes rarely hold without a broader settlement covering the whole country.

A broader Iran war deal remains the clearest route to de-escalation. If Tehran and Riyadh reach an understanding, the Yemen front could be scaled back quickly. The history of the conflict suggests that outside pressure, rather than battlefield outcomes, has been the decisive factor in every pause.

The domestic cost in Saudi Arabia will keep pressure on the leadership. Disrupted daily life and slower non-oil investment make a long campaign harder to sustain quietly. The leadership has tied its international reputation to the Vision 2030 program, and a long war complicates that story.

For shipping and energy markets, the main risk remains the Bab el-Mandeb. Even without a full closure, repeated attacks near the strait can keep freight costs elevated. Every incident near the chokepoint is therefore watched as closely in energy trading rooms as it is in military headquarters.
